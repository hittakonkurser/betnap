Each-way betting explained

An each-way bet is two separate bets of equal size: one on the selection to win, one on it to place. Ask for £5 each-way and you are staking £10. This catches people out more than any other part of a betting slip.

The win half settles as you would expect. The place half pays if the horse finishes inside the places, at a fraction of the odds.

Standard place terms

How many places pay, and at what fraction, depends on the size of the field and whether the race is a handicap. These are the usual terms, though bookmakers extend them for big races as a promotion, so check before betting.

A worked example

Take £5 each-way at 10/1 in a ten-runner non-handicap, so three places at a fifth of the odds. Total stake £10.

ResultWin partPlace partReturnedProfit
Wins£55£15£70+£60
2nd or 3rd—£15£15+£5
Unplaced———−£10

A fifth of 10/1 is 2/1, so the place part returns £10 of winnings plus the £5 stake. Notice how thin the middle row is: the horse ran well enough to place and the bet made £5.

Where the maths turns against you

That thin middle row is not an accident. At a fifth of the odds, the place part only covers both stakes if the price is 5/1 or bigger. At a quarter of the odds, the break-even point is 4/1. Below those prices a placed horse is a losing bet, even though it feels like a result.

This is why each-way is associated with longer-priced runners in big fields, and why backing an odds-on favourite each-way makes little sense. The place half is close to guaranteed to lose money at those prices.
